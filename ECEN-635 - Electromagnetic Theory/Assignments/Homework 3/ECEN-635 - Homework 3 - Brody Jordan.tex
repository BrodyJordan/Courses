\begin{homeworkProblem}
    The field $\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t) = 5\sin(20 \times 10^9 t + kz) \mathbf{\hat{y}} ~ (\unit{V/m})$
    describes a wave propagating in a medium which has the dielectric constant
    $\varepsilon_r = 3$. Answer the following questions concerning this wave. Be sure to
    include dimensions in your answers:

    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), topsep=5pt]
        \item What is the frequency $f$ of the wave?
        \item What is the wave amplitude?
        \item What is the propagation direction?
        \item What is the phase velocity of the wave $v_p$?
        \item What is the wavelength $\lambda$ of the wave?
        \item What is the intrinsic impedance of the wave $\eta$?
        \item What is the wave number (propagation constant) $k$?
    \end{enumerate} \vspace{1\baselineskip}

    \solution

    \textbf{Part (a):} The frequency of the wave $f = \frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{20 \times 10^9}{2\pi} = 3.18$ GHz. \\

    \textbf{Part (b):} The wave amplitude of $\bm{\mathcal{E}}(t)$ is 5 V/m. \\

    \textbf{Part (c):} The propagation direction is in the $\bh{z}$ direction.\\

    \textbf{Part (d):} The phase velocity $v_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3 \cdot \varepsilon_0 \mu_0}} = \qty{1.73e8}{\meter/\second}$ \\

    \textbf{Part (e):} The wavelength of the wave is $\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{\omega \sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{2\pi}{(\qty{20e9}{\radian/\second}) \cdot (\qty{5.78e-9}{\second/\meter})} = \qty{0.0543}{\meter}$ \\

    \textbf{Part (f):} The intrinsic impedance $\eta = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\varepsilon}} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{3\cdot\varepsilon_0}} = \frac{\qty{376.73}{\ohm}}{\sqrt{3}} = \qty{217.51}{\ohm}$ \\

    \textbf{Part (g):} The wave number $k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} = \frac{2\pi}{\qty{0.0543}{\meter}} = \qty{115.71}{\per\meter}$ \\

\end{homeworkProblem}

\begin{homeworkProblem}

    In lossy insulating material a 2.45 GHz plane wave is propagating in a casserole in the $z$-direction
    and polarized in the $y$-direction, with an amplitude of 20 V/m, a conductivity $\sigma = 2.0$ S/m,
    and a dielectric constant $\varepsilon_r = 4$. Find the lost tangent. Hint: this is a trivial question but the
    loss tangent is of primary importance when ordering a material. \\

    \solution

    The loss tangent is given by the formula
    \[
        \tan \delta = \frac{\sigma}{\omega \varepsilon}
    \]
    Plugging in the provided values
    \begin{align*}
        \tan \delta & = \frac{\qty{2.0}{\siemens/\meter}}{(2\pi \times \qty{2.45e9}{\hertz})(4 \cdot \varepsilon_0)}                   \\
                    & = \frac{\qty{2.0}{\siemens/\meter}}{(2\pi \times \qty{2.45e9}{\hertz})(4 \cdot \qty{8.854}{\pico\farad/\meter})} \\
                    & = 3.67 \text{ (dimensionless)}
    \end{align*}


\end{homeworkProblem}

\begin{homeworkProblem}

    The first step in analytically solving for the fields in any situation in electromagnetics is to
    obtain the vector Helmholtz equation from Maxwell's equations. In class, starting with the
    Maxwell's equations the electric field vector Helmholtz equation was derived. Starting with
    Maxwell's equations derive the vector Helmholtz equation for the magnetic field. Write down
    and number Maxwell's equations and at each step in the derivation tell which equation you are
    going to use for the next step. If you use a vector identity, before using it write down the identity
    from those in the back cover of the book. Be sure to use the proper vector symbol and a line
    above the letter to indicate each vector. You had this problem on the last homework but go ahead
    and do it again. \\

    \solution

    We have Maxwell's equations:
    \begin{numcases}{}
        \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -j \omega \mu \mathbf{H} &\quad\quad \\
        \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = j \omega \epsilon \mathbf{E} &\quad\quad \\
        \nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0 &\quad\quad \\
        \nabla \cdot \mathbf{H} = 0 &\quad\quad
    \end{numcases}

    We take the curl of equation (2) to get
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{H}) = \nabla \times (j \omega \epsilon \mathbf{E}) \quad\quad
    \end{equation}
    Then apply the vector identity Appendix II-51 on the left hand side of (5) to get
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{H}) = \nabla(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{H}) - \nabla^2 \mathbf{H} \quad\quad
    \end{equation}
    Next, by substituting (6) into (5), we get
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{H}) - \nabla^2 \mathbf{H} = \nabla \times (j \omega \epsilon \mathbf{E}) = j \omega \epsilon (\nabla \times \mathbf{E}) \quad\quad
    \end{equation}
    Now by applying equations (1) and (4) onto (7), we get
    \begin{equation}
        - \nabla^2 \mathbf{H} = j \omega \epsilon (-j \omega \mu \mathbf{H})
    \end{equation}
    Simplifying (8) by grouping the constants into $k^2$ gives us
    \begin{equation}
        \nabla^2 \mathbf{H} + \omega^2 \mu \epsilon \mathbf{H} = 0 \quad\quad \Rightarrow \quad\quad \nabla^2 \mathbf{H} + k^2 \mathbf{H} = 0
    \end{equation}
    Which is the magnetic field vector Helmholtz equation.

\end{homeworkProblem}

\begin{homeworkProblem}

    In each case below (i) show mathematically that the field given is a straight line, a circle or an
    ellipse (ii) find an expression for the angle $\psi$ between the field vector and the axis. (iii) In each
    case describe the type of polarization. Hint: Remember the polarizaton is found by finding the
    phase of one of field components with respect to the other. A trick to find the difference in phase
    is to add or subtract a phase to both field components. By doing this you can cause the first
    component to have zero phase and the other component will have the difference in the two phases.

    \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), topsep=5pt]
        \item $\mathbf{E} = (j\bh{x} + \bh{y}) e^{-jkz}$
        \item $\mathbf{E} = [(1 + j)\bh{y} + (1-j) \bh{z}] e^{-jkx}$
        \item $\mathbf{E} = [(2 + j)\bh{x} + (3-j) \bh{z}] e^{-jky}$
        \item $\mathbf{E} = (j \bh{x} + j2\bh{y}) e^{-jkz}$
    \end{enumerate}

    Hint: The $\Psi = 0$ axis is determined by the right hand rule. If the wave travels in the $z$ direction then
    $x \rightarrow y \rightarrow z$ says $\Psi = 0$ is the $x$ axis. But if the wave travels in the $y$ direction then
    $z \rightarrow x \rightarrow y$ says $\Psi = 0$ is the $z$ axis.
    \vspace{1\baselineskip}

    \solution

    \textbf{Part (a):} Here $|E_x| = |E_y| = 1$, so they have equal amplitudes. Additionally, their phase difference $\Delta \theta = \pm \pi/2$, meaning the wave is circularly polarized. \\

    \textbf{Part (b):} Now $|E_y| = |E_z| = \sqrt{2}$, so they have equal amplitudes. Additionally, their phase difference $\Delta \theta = \pm \pi/2$, meaning the wave is circularly polarized. \\

    \textbf{Part (c):} Here $|E_x| = \sqrt{5}$ and $|E_z| = \sqrt{10}$. Their phase difference $\Delta \theta = \arctan(1/2) + \arctan(1/3) = \pi/4$, meaning the wave is elliptically polarized. \\

    \textbf{Part (d):} Here $|E_x| = 1$ and $|E_y| = 2$. Their phase difference $\Delta \theta = \pi/2 - \pi/2 = 0$. Thus, the wave is linearly polarized. Then, $\phi = \arctan|E_y|/|E_x| = \arctan(2) = 63.43^\circ$.




\end{homeworkProblem}
